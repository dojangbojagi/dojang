From trusted state to verified execution

  1. Trusted state. A trusted issuer vouches for a fact about your wallet.
  2. Attestation. A sealed fingerprint of the fact is registered on-chain.
  3. Private proof. Your device proves the fact meets a rule without revealing it.
  4. Verified execution. A contract checks the proof and grants access.

Verifiable facts should not require public data.

Most on-chain checks work by publishing the fact itself. That makes the fact easy to trust and impossible to keep private. Private data has the opposite problem: no one else can rely on it.

This protocol pairs the two. A trusted issuer seals a fact in an . You then prove that the sealed fact meets a rule, without showing the fact. The rule is enforced by a smart contract, so anyone can inspect that it was applied.

A vault that checks proofs, not balances.

A smart contract decides who may enter. A valid proof is the key; the number behind it stays private.

What the contract is designed to check, in one transaction

  • The sender is the wallet the proof was made for
  • The commitment is active: from a trusted issuer, not revoked, not expired
  • The policy and threshold match what the contract holds, not what the user supplies
  • The verifier accepts the proof

If any check fails, the transaction reverts and nothing changes.

No deposits, no custody, no yield. The vault only records who has access.
